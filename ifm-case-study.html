Understanding the context

ifm is a global leader in industrial automation sensors and software. Their IoT monitoring platform allows production operators to supervise automated manufacturing systems (tracking metrics, detecting faults, and configuring alerts) in real time.

The Problem

Industrial operators need an efficient and intuitive way to monitor processes and detect issues, because complex, information-heavy monitoring systems with poor workflows lead to slow task completion, cognitive load, errors, and reduced trust in the system.

The goals

  • Keeping setup time for the monitoring wizard to few steps
  • Showing only the most critical information at a glance on the dashboard
  • Integrating an AI assistant that helps without disrupting the operator's workflow

Production operator:

"I just want to set it up and go back to my machines. I shouldn't need to remember which sub-menu hides which setting."

01 - EMPATHIZE

I needed to understand who would use this platform, what their daily work looked like, and what would make a monitoring tool genuinely useful.

Research methods

  • Stakeholder interviews - Spoke to data scientists, developers, product managers, operators and process engineers to understand business goals, technical constraints, and user needs.
  • Competitive benchmarking - Researched how industrial software approached monitoring, identifying what worked and what was over-complicated.
  • Task analysis and mental model mapping - Documented how operators think about monitoring: what information they prioritise, what decisions they make, and in what sequence.

Key research questions:

  • How do operators currently discover issues on the factory floor?
  • What does a typical system configuration session look like end-to-end?
  • Where do operators feel most lost or make the most mistakes?
  • How do they get help when they're stuck?

User persona

Design principles derived from research

  • Urgency first - Critical alerts and KPIs must always be visible
  • Progressive disclosure - Show only what the operator needs right now, details are one tap away
  • Interruption tolerance - Every workflow must survive being abandoned and resume (Auto-save)
  • Guided by default - Configuration flows must guide operators with clear labels (no technical background)
  • Explicit system state - The platform must communicate clearly what it is doing, waiting for, and whether the last action succeeded or failed.

02 - DEFINE

Synthesizing insights into design direction

After clustering research findings using FigJam affinity mapping, three core problem themes emerged. These shaped our "How Might We" statements.

How might we statements

  • How might we design a configuration flow that a non-technical operator can complete independently without errors?
  • How might we display real-time sensor data from dozens of machines so that the most critical issues are always visible at a glance?
  • How might we provide in-context AI assistance without covering or interrupting the live data operators are actively monitoring?

03 - IDEATE

Exploring solutions

I ran three rapid concept sprints over two weeks, one per design track. Here's how ideas evolved from rough sketches to concrete solutions.

Information architecture

Structuring how information should be organized was the first step. I introduced a layered disclosure model that hides details until the operator actively requests it.

Wizard

I mapped the full setup process, then clustered 5 independent steps grouped by dependency. Here's the conceptual flow that won in user testing.

Key design decisions

  • Wizard - A guided wizard - operators are not configuration experts, so a structured linear flow with clear labels reduces cognitive load and prevents incomplete setups.
  • Unified severity colour system - Red/amber/green severity coding was established as a design system primitive, a platform-wide convention, so operators build instant pattern recognition across all three components.
  • Side panel for AI assistant - The AI assistant uses a collapsible side panel. Modals block the live data view, but this panel compresses the dashboard but never hides it.

04 - Design

I built everything in Figma, from rough lo-fi wireframes to hi-fi prototypes handed off to the development team.

05 - Test & iterate

I ran two rounds of usability testing across the project, one on lo-fi wireframes and one on the high-fidelity prototype with operators from two manufacturing facilities.

Key testing findings & what changed:

Finding: Operators misread “Save & Exit” as losing progress.

Fix: Renamed to “Save draft” with a visible auto-save status indicator in the sidebar. Confusion disappeared entirely in retesting.


Finding: Tooltip-based help was never discovered.

Fix: Changed all contextual help to always-visible text beneath field labels, no hover required.


Finding: When the AI panel slid in, operators weren't certain live data was still updating.

Fix: Added a persistent green “LIVE” pulse indicator in the compressed dashboard header.

What I Learned:

  • Designing for a factory floor is fundamentally different from a desk environment. Interruption tolerance and speed of comprehension are not features, they are the product.
  • Understanding development constraints let me make better trade-offs. I stopped proposing interactions that would have cost weeks of build time for marginal usability gain.
  • The three-level disclosure model was the most impactfull decision in the project.
